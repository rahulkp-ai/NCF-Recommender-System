# Chapter 3 — Literature Review and Research Gap

## Chapter Overview

This chapter provides a critical review of the literature underpinning the present study, tracing the methodological evolution of recommender systems from early memory-based collaborative filtering through latent-factor matrix factorization to deep learning-based and neural collaborative filtering approaches, before turning to hybrid recommendation, cold-start mitigation, evaluation methodology, and production recommendation systems. Each body of work is assessed not merely for what it proposes but for its methodology, the datasets and evaluation protocols it uses, its demonstrated strengths, and its acknowledged limitations. The chapter concludes by synthesising these threads into an explicit statement of the research gap this thesis addresses and by positioning the present study, including its specific implementational scope, within that gap. Citation numbering introduced in this chapter follows on from Chapters 1 and 2 and will be reconciled into a single, final numbering scheme in the References chapter, as noted in the Evidence Gaps section below.

## 3.1 Evolution of Recommender Systems

Recommender systems research has developed through successive methodological generations, each responding to a specific limitation of its predecessor. Early systems relied on memory-based collaborative filtering, computing similarities directly between users or items from the raw interaction matrix. As catalogue and user-base sizes grew, the quadratic-in-population cost of memory-based similarity computation motivated a shift toward model-based approaches, most prominently latent-factor matrix factorization, which achieved substantial visibility through its dominant role in the Netflix Prize competition [2]. Since approximately 2015, a further generation of methods has applied deep neural network architectures to the recommendation problem, motivated by the representational limitations of the fixed inner-product interaction function used by matrix factorization, and by the empirical successes of deep learning in adjacent domains such as computer vision and natural language processing. Neural Collaborative Filtering [1] is a central contribution within this most recent generation, and its formulation, implementation, and verification form the mathematical and experimental core of this thesis (Chapters 2, 6, and 7).

This three-generation trajectory — memory-based, latent-factor, neural — is not merely a chronological account. Each transition addressed a specific, well-documented limitation of the preceding paradigm: memory-based methods do not scale; latent-factor methods scale but impose a restrictive linear interaction function; neural methods relax this restriction at the cost of reduced interpretability and increased sensitivity to hyperparameter choices and initialisation. This chapter reviews representative literature at each stage, with particular depth applied to the neural generation, since it is the generation within which this thesis's implementation is situated.

## 3.2 Traditional Collaborative Filtering

Item-based collaborative filtering, as formalised by Sarwar et al. [3], computes similarities between items from co-occurrence patterns in the user–item interaction matrix and generates recommendations by aggregating a user's known preferences for items similar to unrated candidates. The methodology in [3] is evaluated using explicit MovieLens rating data, and the paper demonstrates that item-based similarity computation offers substantially better scalability than user-based approaches, since item–item similarity matrices are more stable over time (permitting offline precomputation) than the corresponding user–user matrices in most consumer catalogues. This scalability advantage was a primary reason item-based methods were adopted in early large-scale commercial recommender deployments.

The limitation of memory-based methods most relevant to the present study is structural rather than empirical: because predictions are computed directly from the sparse interaction matrix at query time (or from a precomputed similarity matrix derived from it), memory-based methods have no mechanism for learning a compact, generalisable representation of a user or item independent of the exact rows and columns observed during training. This absence of a learned latent representation is precisely the property that model-based methods, reviewed next, were designed to provide, and it is also the property — an explicit, trainable embedding representation — that both the classical matrix-factorization literature and the neural literature reviewed below share and that the present study's implementation (Chapter 6) directly relies upon.

## 3.3 Matrix Factorization Approaches

Matrix factorization approaches represent users and items as learned latent vectors in a shared low-dimensional space and predict interaction strength via the inner product of these vectors, as established in Section 2.2.3. Koren, Bell, and Volinsky [2] provide the most widely cited synthesis of this paradigm, motivated substantially by its success in the Netflix Prize competition, and demonstrate that matrix factorization models — which incorporate biases, confidence weighting for implicit feedback, and optionally temporal dynamics — consistently outperform classical nearest-neighbour techniques for explicit-rating prediction at scale. A separate, earlier probabilistic formulation of the same underlying idea, Probabilistic Matrix Factorization, was proposed by Salakhutdinov and Mnih [9], which treats the latent factorisation as a generative probabilistic model and demonstrates favourable performance on large, sparse rating matrices, including subsets of the Netflix dataset, relative to standard singular value decomposition approaches.

For implicit-feedback settings specifically — the setting relevant to this thesis (Section 2.1.3) — Rendle et al. [4] introduce Bayesian Personalised Ranking (BPR), a pairwise ranking-optimisation criterion that trains a matrix-factorization (or other latent-factor) model to directly maximise the probability that observed items are ranked above unobserved items for a given user, rather than optimising a pointwise regression or classification loss. BPR's methodological contribution — framing implicit-feedback recommendation as ranking optimisation rather than rating-value regression — is conceptually related to, but distinct from, the pointwise BCE-based training objective adopted by NCF [1] and by the present study's implementation (Section 2.4.5); this distinction is a relevant axis of comparison discussed further in Section 3.10.

The methodological common thread across [2], [9], and [4] is the representation of users and items via learned latent vectors combined by a fixed algebraic interaction function (inner product, in all three cases). This is precisely the representational bottleneck that He et al. [1] identify and that motivates the neural generalisation reviewed next; the present thesis's mathematical formulation (Section 2.2.3) and its motivating discussion of the inner product's limitations (Section 1.6, 3.10) are drawn directly from this line of argument.

## 3.4 Deep Learning-Based Recommendation

Beyond the specific NCF architecture examined in depth in this thesis, deep learning has been applied to recommendation through several distinct architectural strategies. Wide & Deep Learning, proposed by Cheng et al. [7], jointly trains a linear ("wide") model over cross-product feature transformations, intended to memorise specific, frequently co-occurring feature combinations, and a deep feedforward neural network ("deep") over dense embeddings of categorical features, intended to generalise to previously unseen feature combinations. The methodology in [7] is evaluated in a production application-recommendation setting (the Google Play app store) using live online A/B testing in addition to offline metrics, and it demonstrates that the joint wide-and-deep formulation improves app acquisition rate relative to either the wide-only or deep-only components in isolation. This is architecturally distinct from NCF: Wide & Deep combines memorisation and generalisation pathways over engineered cross-features, whereas NCF's GMF and MLP pathways (Section 2.4) both operate over learned embeddings without explicit feature engineering.

Covington, Adams, and Sargin [8] describe the YouTube video recommendation system's deep candidate-generation and ranking networks, in which a deep neural network consumes aggregated watch-history and search-history embeddings, alongside example age and other contextual features, to generate a ranked candidate set at massive industrial scale. The methodology in [8] is notable for its explicit discussion of engineering constraints — training data freshness, serving latency, and example-age bias correction — that are largely absent from academic offline-benchmark papers, including [1]. This industrial orientation is directly relevant to the research-to-production continuity gap articulated in Section 3.9 and Section 3.11 below, since [8] demonstrates a deployed system but, consistent with common practice in industrial systems papers, does not expose a from-scratch mathematical derivation or independent gradient-verification methodology for its underlying model, in contrast to the emphasis of the present thesis (Chapter 6).

More recent work has extended neural recommendation beyond feedforward architectures to graph-based representation learning. Wang et al. [6] propose Neural Graph Collaborative Filtering (NGCF), which explicitly models the user–item interaction graph and propagates embeddings along its edges using graph convolution operations, under the argument that a purely feedforward interaction function (as used by NCF [1] and by the MLP-based variant implemented in this thesis) does not explicitly encode the higher-order connectivity structure of the interaction graph — for example, the fact that two users are connected via a shared set of interacted items. NGCF is evaluated on the Gowalla, Yelp2018, and Amazon-Book datasets using Recall@K and NDCG@K, and demonstrates improvements over NCF-style feedforward baselines, attributed specifically to the explicit modelling of multi-hop connectivity information that pure embedding-concatenation architectures do not capture. This graph-based line of work represents a natural extension beyond the scope of the present thesis and is discussed as a candidate future research direction in Chapter 15.

## 3.5 Neural Collaborative Filtering

Neural Collaborative Filtering, proposed by He et al. [1], is the foundational reference for the model examined in this thesis and merits closer methodological treatment than the works surveyed above. He et al. formally establish, via a worked counter-example, that the inner product — the fixed interaction function used by classical matrix factorization [2] — cannot represent arbitrary rankings of items for a user once latent dimensionality is constrained, and they propose replacing it with a learned interaction function realised through the GMF and MLP pathways described in Section 2.4, fused into the NeuMF architecture. Their evaluation methodology, conducted on the MovieLens and Pinterest datasets under a leave-one-out protocol with Hit Ratio@10 and NDCG@10 as ranking metrics, is the direct methodological template adopted by the present study's own evaluation protocol (Chapter 4, Chapter 8): both the leave-one-out split strategy and the Hit Rate/NDCG metric pairing used in this thesis's experimental chapters are drawn from this source.

A methodologically important, and frequently under-examined, aspect of He et al.'s work is the degree of transparency around implementation-level correctness. As is standard practice for papers building on established deep learning frameworks (in the original paper's case, Keras with a Theano backend, per the authors' released code), gradient correctness is implicit in the framework's automatic differentiation engine and is not independently reported or verified as an experimental result within the paper itself. This is not a methodological flaw specific to [1] — it reflects a near-universal convention across the neural recommendation literature, evident also in [6], [7], and [8] — but it is precisely the convention that the present thesis's gradient-verification methodology (Section 2.5, Chapter 6) departs from, by deriving and numerically verifying backpropagation independently of any automatic differentiation framework.

A second point of methodological comparison concerns architectural completeness. As established in Chapter 1 and Chapter 2, He et al.'s complete NeuMF architecture fuses a GMF pathway (an element-wise product over one embedding pair) with an MLP pathway (a concatenation-based feedforward network over a second embedding pair). The implementation examined in this thesis (Chapters 6 and 7) realises only the MLP pathway, using a single shared embedding table rather than the dual-pathway, dual-embedding NeuMF fusion. This is an important scope boundary for situating the present study's contribution relative to [1]: the thesis investigates, implements, and empirically validates the MLP-based collaborative filtering variant of the NCF framework, not the complete NeuMF fusion architecture, and this distinction is maintained explicitly throughout the empirical chapters rather than being elided.

## 3.6 Hybrid Recommendation Systems

The theoretical basis for hybrid recommendation, combining collaborative, content-based, and popularity signals, was introduced in Section 2.6. The literature on hybrid recommendation is extensive and spans multiple distinct hybridisation strategies — weighted linear combination, switching between models based on a selection criterion, feature-level combination (as in Wide & Deep [7], which can itself be read as a hybrid of memorisation and generalisation pathways), and cascade or meta-level combination. The specific hybridisation strategy implemented in the present study (Chapter 9) — an interaction-count-dependent weighted blend of an NCF collaborative score and a combined popularity/content cold-start score — is architecturally a weighted-combination hybrid with a data-dependent mixing coefficient, conceptually related to switching hybrids in the limiting cases where the mixing coefficient approaches 0 or 1 (Section 2.7.3).

A methodologically salient observation is that comparative, quantitative evaluation of hybrid strategies against their constituent single-signal baselines (pure collaborative filtering, pure content-based, pure popularity) is common in the literature but was not conducted as part of the present study: as established in the project audit preceding this thesis, no popularity-only or content-only baseline Hit Rate/NDCG evaluation was executed against the trained NCF model in this project's experimental artifacts. Consequently, while the present study demonstrates the hybrid engine's qualitative branching behaviour empirically (Chapter 9), it does not, and cannot, claim a quantitative accuracy improvement of the hybrid strategy over a pure-NCF or pure-baseline alternative, in contrast to the quantitative hybrid-versus-baseline comparisons that are more typically reported in the dedicated hybrid-recommendation literature. This is identified explicitly as part of the research gap in Section 3.11 and revisited as a limitation in Chapter 14.

## 3.7 Cold-Start Recommendation

The cold-start problem, formalised theoretically in Section 2.7, has been addressed in the literature through several distinct strategies beyond the popularity/content hybrid approach adopted in this thesis, including meta-learning approaches that learn to rapidly adapt a model to a new user or item from very few examples, and cross-domain approaches that transfer collaborative signal from a source domain with denser interaction data. A full comparative treatment of this broader cold-start literature is beyond the empirical scope of the present study, since the implemented cold-start mechanism (Chapter 9) is restricted to the popularity/content weighted-blend strategy described in Section 2.6.4 and Section 2.7.3.

Within the boundary of what was actually implemented, the relevant comparison point is the general design principle — articulated across the hybrid-recommendation literature — that content-based and popularity-based signals require no accumulated collaborative interaction history and are therefore natural fallback signals when collaborative embeddings are undertrained or absent, precisely the design principle realised by the hybrid engine examined in Chapter 9. The present study's contribution relative to this literature is not a novel cold-start algorithm, but rather the integration and empirical demonstration (via executed notebook cells, per the project audit) of a specific, functioning instance of this well-established design principle, operating over the specific NCF model developed and validated in Chapters 6–8.

## 3.8 Recommender System Evaluation

Two distinct evaluation traditions coexist in the recommender-systems literature, corresponding to the rating-prediction and top-K ranking formulations distinguished in Section 2.1.4. Rating-prediction evaluation, using RMSE or MAE, was the dominant protocol during the matrix-factorization era exemplified by [2], reflecting that era's focus on Netflix Prize-style explicit-rating datasets. Top-K ranking evaluation, using Hit Ratio, Precision@K, Recall@K, and NDCG@K, has become the dominant protocol for implicit-feedback neural recommendation research, exemplified by [1], [6], and adopted by the present study.

A specific methodological detail carried over from [1] into the present study's own evaluation protocol (Chapter 4, Chapter 8) is the leave-one-out train/validation/test split, in which each user's single most recent interaction is held out for testing, the second-most-recent for validation, and the remainder used for training. This protocol has the practical advantage of preserving one evaluable instance per user regardless of that user's total interaction count, but it has a documented limitation, acknowledged across the literature: full ranking over the entire item catalogue at evaluation time is computationally expensive, and consequently many implementations, including — per the project audit underlying this thesis — the present study's own scratch-model validation procedure, restrict Hit Rate computation to a random user subsample (200 users, per the audited configuration) rather than the full user population, with the single full-catalogue, full-user-population evaluation reserved for the PyTorch implementation's dedicated test-set run. This distinction between subsample-based and full-population evaluation is a methodological detail of direct relevance to the comparability of the specific numerical results reported in Chapter 8, and is discussed there and revisited in Chapter 14 as a threat to the internal validity of the scratch-versus-PyTorch comparison.

## 3.9 Production Recommendation Systems

The industrial systems literature, exemplified by Covington et al. [8] and, to a lesser extent, Cheng et al. [7], addresses concerns largely absent from purely academic offline-benchmark papers: serving latency, feature freshness, training-serving skew, and the engineering architecture required to operate a recommendation model at scale. These papers are methodologically distinct from academic NCF-style papers in that their primary contribution is a deployed system architecture and its measured production impact (e.g., via online A/B testing), rather than an offline accuracy benchmark on a static, public dataset.

The present study's production-oriented chapters (Chapters 10–12) draw on this systems-literature tradition for architectural framing — the separation of a model-serving layer from a request-handling backend, the use of containerisation and orchestration for deployment, and the instrumentation of the running system with metrics and dashboards — while explicitly not attempting to replicate the industrial scale, online-experimentation methodology, or measured production impact (e.g., latency-under-load, live A/B test results) reported in papers such as [8]. As established in the project audit, no production load-testing or online-evaluation data exists within this project's artifacts; the production architecture is evaluated on the basis of its implemented code, container configuration, and test suite, not on measured production performance, a scope boundary maintained explicitly in Chapters 10–12 and revisited in Chapter 14.

## 3.11 Identified Research Gap

Synthesising Sections 3.1 through 3.10, three specific and jointly under-addressed gaps in the reviewed literature can be stated precisely, corresponding directly to the research questions articulated in Section 1.8.

**Gap 1 — Verification methodology.** As Table 3.1 makes explicit, no reviewed work reports independent, implementation-level verification of gradient correctness for its neural interaction function; correctness is uniformly delegated to the automatic differentiation engine of the framework in use. This is a reasonable and standard engineering practice, but it means the literature offers little direct evidence, and correspondingly little worked methodological example, of how one would verify by hand that a manually derived backpropagation implementation for a neural recommendation model is in fact correct. RQ1 addresses this gap directly.

**Gap 2 — From-scratch/framework parity.** No reviewed work provides a controlled, matched-hyperparameter comparison between a hand-derived (non-framework) implementation of a neural recommendation model and a framework-based implementation of the architecturally equivalent model, evaluated jointly on convergence behaviour, recommendation quality, and computational cost. Such a comparison is of direct pedagogical and diagnostic value — it is the only way to empirically distinguish genuine architectural or optimisation differences from confounds introduced by tooling — yet it does not appear as a standard component of the reviewed NCF literature. RQ1 and RQ2 address this gap.

**Gap 3 — Research-to-production continuity.** The academic literature on neural recommendation ([1], [6]) is evaluated almost exclusively via offline benchmarks and rarely extends into a documented, testable, and observable production architecture. Conversely, the industrial systems literature ([7], [8]) documents production architecture and, in some cases, online production impact, but — consistent with standard industrial publication practice — does not expose the underlying model's mathematical derivation, from-scratch implementation, or gradient-verification methodology in comparable depth to academic papers. Few reviewed works bridge both traditions within a single, methodologically transparent study. RQ3 and RQ4 address this gap.

A further, narrower observation from Section 3.6 is that quantitative hybrid-versus-single-signal baseline comparison, common in the dedicated hybrid-recommendation literature, was not conducted within the present study's own experimental artifacts (only qualitative/behavioural demonstration was performed); this is noted here as a boundary on how strongly the present study's own hybrid contribution can be positioned relative to that literature, and is revisited as an explicit limitation in Chapter 14 rather than being presented as a fully closed gap.

## 3.12 Positioning of the Present Study

The present study is positioned at the intersection of Gaps 1, 2, and 3 identified in Section 3.11. It contributes: (i) a from-scratch NumPy implementation of the MLP-based NCF interaction function with independently verified backpropagation for its output layer, directly addressing Gap 1 within the scope of what was tested; (ii) a matched-hyperparameter empirical comparison between this from-scratch implementation and an architecturally equivalent PyTorch implementation, evaluated on training convergence, validation Hit Rate, and wall-clock training time, directly addressing Gap 2; and (iii) a documented, containerised, tested, and Prometheus/Grafana-observed production architecture that operationalises the trained model alongside a hybrid cold-start recommendation engine, addressing Gap 3, albeit — as established in Section 3.9 and revisited in Chapter 14 — without the online experimentation or production-load evidence available to industrial systems papers such as [8].

Two scope boundaries, established in Chapters 1 and 2 and reaffirmed here in light of the literature reviewed in this chapter, are worth restating explicitly at the close of this review. First, the implemented and empirically evaluated architecture realises the MLP pathway of the general NCF framework [1] in isolation, without the GMF pathway or the NeuMF fusion; the present study's contribution to Gap 1 and Gap 2 is therefore scoped to this MLP-only variant and should not be read as a verification or parity study of the complete NeuMF architecture. Second, the gradient verification performed (Chapter 6) covers the output-layer weight via a single numerical-differentiation test and does not extend to the embedding layers or hidden layers individually; the contribution to Gap 1 is accordingly a demonstration of verification methodology and a scoped correctness result, not a claim of exhaustive, layer-by-layer gradient certification. These boundaries are maintained consistently through Chapters 6, 7, 13, and 14.

## Chapter Summary

This chapter has critically reviewed the literature spanning memory-based collaborative filtering, matrix factorization, deep learning-based and neural recommendation, hybrid and cold-start strategies, evaluation methodology, and production recommendation systems, comparing each reviewed work's methodology, dataset, strengths, and limitations rather than merely summarising algorithmic mechanics. The review identifies three specific, jointly under-addressed gaps — implementation-level gradient verification, controlled from-scratch/framework parity comparison, and research-to-production continuity — and positions the present study explicitly within these gaps, while stating clearly the scope boundaries (MLP-only architecture; output-layer-only gradient verification; no online production evaluation) within which that positioning holds. This positioning is carried forward directly into Chapter 4's methodology and Chapter 13's interpretation of findings against the research questions stated in Section 1.8.
